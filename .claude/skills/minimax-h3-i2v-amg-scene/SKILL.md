# MiniMax H3 I2V: AMG Physique Film (Bob Mizer)

Build 2026-10-18-1845

> ## ⚠️ THIS RUN WRITES EXACTLY {{COUNT}} SHOT(S): READ FIRST
>
> Output **exactly {{COUNT}}** prompts, one per shot, as `<<<PROMPT N.M>>>` blocks, no more, no fewer. {{COUNT}} is always **3 or more**; single shots are not supported, so a value below 3 is treated as 3. **Never collapse the film into one shot.** Before you finish, count your prompt blocks and add any that are missing. {{COUNT}} always wins.

## Vocabulary

Hierarchy: **beat > shot > scene > film.**

- **BEAT**: one move inside a shot (a turn, a pose struck, a hand path, a hold).
- **SHOT**: one 10-second clip, one continuous camera run. Three prose phases, each holding beats.
- **SCENE**: three shots generated together as one video. Scene 1 is the front scene, scene 2 the back scene, scene 3 the calisthenics scene; the cycle continues with scenes 4, 5…
- **FILM**: the whole output, all scenes. `{{COUNT}}` is the number of shots in the film.
- **BLOCKING**: the planned beats inside a shot, written as timed lines for the director; what `scenes/scene-N/shot-M.txt` records.
- **TAKE**: one generation of a scene. Takes of a scene share its keyframe and differ by the random seed; the owner draws several per scene. A "draw" in the evidence lines is a take.
- **SHOT LIST**: the table of every shot in the film, `shot-list.md`.
- **KEYFRAME**: the starting image a scene's motion begins from, `keyframes/scene-N.png`, supplied by the owner.

The bracketed `[Shot 1]` inside the description is H3's own marker for a camera run, not this skill's shot. Every shot here is one locked run, so it is always `[Shot 1]`. Where a quoted H3 prompt string says "clip" ("Through the middle of the clip", "as the clip ends"), that is the tested wording the model sees; leave it verbatim.

You are writing **finished MiniMax-H3 I2VA prompts**, one per shot, that continue a keyframe as a Bob Mizer / Athletic Model Guild physique film. The keyframe is a still from a modelling session and the shot is the film of that same session: no plot, a model working through his material for whoever is watching. **Motion is the erotic content.** The model uses the body, then looks up at the camera, and that awareness is the charge.

**The job: arouse the viewer, coded as an AMG reel.** The coding is how Mizer did it: a plausible pretext (a pose, a feat, a prop, a task) whose real purpose is what it lets the viewer look at. Every shot passes two tests. **The pretext test:** Mizer could have filmed it as a physique reel; no bedroom, no plot, no sex act. **The ceiling:** no sex act, and the genitals never appear. The garment stays on the body only because video generation cannot remove it cleanly, so everything short of removal is in play: the band slid down as far as it goes in front with the V and the hip crease fully clear, the band pushed off the glutes entirely behind, the leg opening hitched high on the thigh, fabric pulled taut by a stance or a stretch so the shape under it reads. The hand pulls the band and never into it, and never settles at the groin. **The payoff test:** name, in a few words, what the viewer gets that the frame before withheld: the band lower on the hips, glutes clenched under taut fabric, the man stepping close and finally looking at him. No nameable payoff means a fitness demo: rewrite it. Payoffs escalate, each bigger than the last in its scene, the final shot of the film's the biggest in the film.

## How H3 is different, and why the prompt looks the way it does

**What you write is exactly what the model sees.** The cloud runs prompts through a rewriter; the local model has none, so you produce the finished prompt in H3's native format and it is sent unchanged.

- **The format is fixed.** Line 1 is the alignment line, verbatim. Then a blank line, then three labelled fields. The description is ONE paragraph opening with `[Shot 1]`, no headings, no line breaks, no bracketed times inside it.
- **Timestamps mean cuts.** In H3's grammar a timecode only ever introduces a new camera run (`[Shot 2] At 00:03.500, the camera cuts to…`). Never write `[0:00-0:03]`, `[0s-3s]` or `at the 7-second mark`. The three phases are written as **prose**: *In the first seconds… Through the middle of the clip… In the final seconds… In the final second…* They land in order every time.
- **Counts are not honoured; order and shape are.** A move gets about two seconds and the holds stretch to fill. "Three steps", "an inch", a hip drop are harmless but unreliable; a pose written as a shape with the hands placed (fists at the back of the waist, fists by the ears, palm flat on the pec) is what lands. Put the pacing in the order of events and the length of the holds, never in numbers.
- **The anchor holds the set and the man.** A substantial anchor paragraph (the first frame described exactly) is what keeps the set, the garment and the face from drifting: the set and the man held 14 of 14 (9 to 10 Oct 2026, Equinox stack, all with anchors). But the anchor describes a STATE, never a pose and never anything that will change: a towel, a hand position or a stance written into the anchor fights every later beat.
- **The keyframe's starting state carries over until a beat releases it.** Hands clasped behind the back in the photo stayed clasped through a "lat spread" because no beat unclasped them. Phase one of a scene's first shot always names what the keyframe's hands and feet are doing and moves them.
- **Every beat's end shape must fit inside the keyframe's frame.** H3 does not keep the frame for you: an action that goes above the frame moves the camera (a push-in and tilt, 2 of 2 with the static-camera sentence present), and an action that goes below it is cut off (a deep squat on a mid-thigh keyframe scored 3/3/2 against 3/4/4 for the same squat with two steps back first; 11 Oct 2026, 12 draws on Equinox). So before writing a beat, check the keyframe's crop and headroom: a reach or an arm overhead needs headroom above the head (above the hat if he wears one); a squat, fold or hinge needs the glutes and knees above the bottom edge at full depth. **The rule covers distance too, because distance is size:** a walk toward the lens makes him bigger, a step away makes him smaller, and H3 honours the walk while ignoring framing words that say where to stop. So judge the END size of any move toward or away from the camera against the keyframe's headroom and crop before writing it. If the end shape would leave the frame, either add distance first, a step or two away from the camera in the scene's first shot, to a place its keyframe already shows, which H3 draws cleanly, or shorten the move, or play the beat in place. This applies to every kind, every scene and every beat, **and nothing in this skill overrides it, the subscriber close included.**
- **Check what the keyframe puts under him before any move that lowers him.** Before writing a squat, a bend, a seat or any move that drops the hips, look at what the keyframe shows directly under and behind his legs. H3 takes whatever is there as the ground: on a keyframe whose lower legs overlap a pool's tile band, a deep squat sank him into the water, tilted the camera down and redrew the set at the next join in 3 of 4 takes, and the one take that stayed on the deck shipped (16 Oct 2026, Equinox). The join breaks because the extension's anchor, written from words, no longer matches the frame the squat ended in. If the keyframe shows anything other than plain floor under his feet (water, a step, a ledge, a drop), keep the move standing, or tell the owner the keyframe needs the floor clearly under his feet before writing the scene. Never write the anchor as standing somewhere the picture does not show.
- **No kneeling, nothing lying on the ground.** A kneel landed 0 of 5 (failed 5 of 5) in a locked frame (9 to 10 Oct 2026, Equinox). Seated work is fine: a man sitting on sand or a ledge drew every seated move cleanly in 7 of 7 takes (knees opening, a chest arch, palms on the knees, a seated front double biceps; 2 shipped, none failed for the seat). Kneeling, lying down and hands planted on the ground stay banned. **The squat is fine:** it drew 5 of 5 in the back round, and the deepest squat that stayed in frame was the highest-scored shot. So nothing in this skill puts a knee or a hand on the ground, but a squat written as a shape (feet placed wide, hips sinking toward the heels, chest up, hands on the hips) is a HINGED kind and welcome. A lunge is written with the feet placed or it becomes a squat.
- **A turn toward the camera brings back the photo's front view.** Useful for the final shot of the film, fatal for the back scene: a back shot that turns to face the lens ends as a front shot. Back-scene shots stay turned away and look over the shoulder.
- **The walk-in is a frame-rule case.** On a keyframe with headroom it works (head-to-thigh to waist-up, 9 of 9, camera still locked). On a keyframe with almost no headroom the mandated step-in walked his face out of the top of the frame in 3 of 3 draws, and the same close played in place stayed in frame 9 of 9 (11 to 12 Oct 2026, Equinox); the step back planned before the walk often did not draw, so the walk started close. Judge the keyframe first: a clear band above the head (above the hat if he wears one), roughly a head's height or more (a rule of thumb, untested), and the walk-in fits; less, and the close plays in place. The camera is not a set element, so a walk-in that fits is unaffected by the next rule.
- **Travel lives only in a scene's first shot, and only to a place the keyframe already shows. Shots 2 and 3 stay where shot 1 ended.** An extension sees no picture, so any travel in it is drawn from words alone and H3 redraws the set to match them instead of continuing the frame. Since unpinning (10 to 13 Oct 2026, Equinox), scripts with travel cut or changed the set in 7 of 15 takes (a walk to a far prop 4 of 6, a step back into a black void 1 of 6, a change of depth in water 2 of 3); in-place scripts did so in 1 of about 27. So a scene's first shot may walk, step back or wade, but only to somewhere visible in its keyframe, and it ends there; shots 2 and 3 brace, lean, pose and work in place, with no travel beyond a step to set the feet. Two moves are not travel: the step-in toward the lens in a scene's last shot, when the frame rule allows it, and a single sideways step or turn into the final pinup. When the keyframe makes a move likely (waist-deep water, so he sits on a ledge), write that move on purpose in shot 1: the written seat held the 10 s join 3 of 3 where the unwritten one cut 2 of 3. The pin is a fallback switch, not the default: pinned rounds still cut once in about 140 takes and snapped the pose back at the 20 s join in 12 of 12 takes that changed pose.
- **The end-hold sentence is honoured.** "…and holds there as the clip ends" kept the scripted end pose 14 of 14. Every shot ends on that sentence.
- **The camera sentence locks the camera** 14 of 14 when written in full. Omit it and the guide says H3 picks a dolly (from the guide, untested; never omit it). Write "ten-second" everywhere, never "twelve-second".
- **Clothing stays on only when the anchor says it stays on** (25 of 25 with the sentence, 10 to 11 Oct 2026). Off-frame reveals fail: a waist crop rendered without the trunks named for it. Prefer actions that stay inside what the keyframe shows.
- **Hands with small objects and liquids fail.** Towels fumbled in every draw across two rounds, and scooping or throwing water is a harder ask. Props are only large and fixed: a column, a rail, a wall, a bench, a doorframe, the surf itself. Nothing handheld, nothing poured, nothing scooped. With water, the default is to walk INTO it and let it act on him (the water reaching a named part of his body, a wave or current moving past him); a scoop-and-throw is an opt-in experiment, never the default.
- **Audio is drawn with the picture.** Two fields follow the description: `overall_soundscape` (diegetic, in time order) and `non_diegetic_music` (`None.`). There are no voices in these films, so no dialogue tags; say so in the soundscape.
- **There is no negative prompt.** Write what IS present. One short exclusion inside the soundscape (`no voices, no music`) is fine; a list of forbidden visuals is not.
- **Name the subject by appearance in every phase**: "the <one or two distinguishing features> man in the <garment as the photo shows it>". Never a bare pronoun across the paragraph, never a name. The face held 14 of 14 this way.
- **Ten seconds per shot**; the frame count is the pipeline's business. Write `ten-second` in the camera sentence.
- **Each scene is its own generation.** The first shot of every scene sees its own keyframe: it gets the alignment line, `<Picture 1>` in the camera sentence, an anchor written from that keyframe, and a phase one that releases that keyframe's hands and feet. It is never written as an extension of the previous scene, because scene N's keyframe is supplied by the owner and may differ from scene N minus 1's last frame. **Shots 2 and 3 of a scene are extensions and see no picture.** Each begins at `integrated_multimodal_description:`, its camera sentence says "the man, the set and the lighting stay as they are" with no picture reference, its anchor describes the previous shot's end frame as fact, and its action opens with the carry-over opener: `For the first moment the <man by appearance> holds where he stopped, <the previous shot's exact end pose>, then moves.` That opener held every join, 14 of 14.
- **Two moves per phase, maximum.** H3 gives each move about two seconds and keeps order, not duration, so a phase with four or five moves drops some. Count the moves in every phase; release-and-set counts as one, a hand path as one, a pose as one.
- **The walk-in, when the keyframe allows it, gets its own phase.** Head-to-thigh to waist-up used an entire phase in 9 of 9 draws. It never shares a phase with a flex or a hand path: from the house shot it takes the final phase and the look lands as he sets; from further back it takes the middle phase and the final phase carries the pose.

## What the keyframe decides

The prompt can never add what the frame lacks, and H3 regenerates the frame from your words after the first instant, so the keyframe decides more here than on any other target.

- **A worked body.** Flush, sweat, wet hair, veins up. A dry, rested body caps the heat, and no wording recovers it.
- **Wardrobe, worn modern, and named in the anchor exactly as the photo shows it, as staying on.** The garment held 25 of 25 when the anchor matched the photo word for word; a cut the photo does not show ("square-cut" on a swim brief) invites a redraw. Briefs, trunks, compression shorts, a jock, a low-slung towel already worn. The garment must not cover the body. A mid-film costume change is a keyframe job: supply the next scene's keyframe in the new garment, never write the change.
- **An open, unguarded face.** H3 holds the keyframe's face; a stone-faced keyframe gives stone-faced shots.
- **Framing: medium full is the house shot** (knees-up, headroom). Full is fine when the action travels. Never tighter than waist-up, never a face close-up: punch in during the edit. Leave room in the direction the action goes, because H3 will move the camera or cut the body off rather than recentre (see *Every beat's end shape must fit inside the keyframe's frame*).
- **The floor under his feet.** For any scene with a squat, a bend or a seat, the keyframe must show the floor he stands on (the deck, sand, stone) clearly under and around his feet, with water or edges beside or behind him, not under his legs. A crop just above the ankles is fine when the floor is plainly visible beneath him.
- **Aspect and resolution are set here.** H3 output is 1344×768-class; a keyframe of another shape is squashed, not cropped, which distorts faces (from the guide, untested). Match the output shape in whatever generates the stills.
- **A simple backdrop, large fixed props only.** A column, a rail, a wall, a bench, the surf, a doorway. Nothing handheld, nothing small.

## The film

You are given `{{COUNT}}`, always 3 or more, and one keyframe per scene: `keyframes/scene-1.png`, `keyframes/scene-2.png`, and so on, supplied by the owner. Each shot is exactly 10 seconds, so total runtime is `{{COUNT}} × 10`. Shots are grouped into scenes of three; inside a scene the three shots are ONE continuous take: shot 1 starts from the scene's keyframe, and shots 2 and 3 are each generated from the previous shot's final frame. So within a scene each shot's ending is the next shot's first frame, and the next shot's anchor describes THAT frame (the position the last shot landed in). Between scenes nothing carries over in the prompt: the next scene starts from its own keyframe.

**Every shot is a physically believable continuation that displays the body through a real gesture that travels** (a pose struck, a prop worked, a turn completed, a walk, a hand path), never a breath or a settle alone.

## Read the keyframe for heat first

Everything after this section is structure, and structure alone produces the same film every time. Before choosing anything, list eight to twelve ways THIS body could be displayed in THIS frame that would make someone stop scrolling. Every candidate is a physique display (a body presented, worked, braced, driven, held), never merely an interesting thing a man could do in the room. Go wide and specific: what can he brace against, lean into, step up to, walk into? What does each do to the garment and to the light already in the frame? Rank by two tests: which is hottest, and which could ONLY have come from this keyframe. Keep the top four or five; those are the blocks, and the kinds fall out of them. They are written into the shot list before any prompt. Three guards on every candidate action, and nothing else constrains the list: the action uses something in the frame or just outside it; the action is still a physique display, with the body as the content; and the action stays under the ceiling (see *The job*).

## Building the film: the scenes

A film is built in SCENES of three shots, always in this order.

| Scene | Carries | Facing | Every shot but the final shot of the film ends |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1, Front** | the front of the body: poses, props, braces | front → three-quarter → side | face square, eyes in the lens |
| **2, Back** | the back and glutes | away, rear three-quarter, the whole scene | still turned away, head over the shoulder, eyes in the lens |
| **3, Calisthenics** | standing bodyweight work, reps as the gesture | whatever angle shows the move | eyes in the lens, square or over the shoulder to match |
| **4, 5, …** | the cycle repeats: front, then back, then calisthenics | | |

Scenes fill in order and the last may be short: `{{COUNT}}` 3 is the front scene alone, 4 or 5 adds a one or two shot back scene, 7 or 8 a short calisthenics scene. Each scene ramps to its last shot, each scene opens above where the previous one opened, and the final shot of the film is the peak.

**Fail fast: the back scene's riskiest beat goes in its first shot.** A scene is generated one shot at a time, so a take can be judged and cancelled after its first ten seconds. In the back scene, put the beat most likely to fail in shot N.1: the glimpse (see *The back scene*) and any new or untested move with it. The front scene does not need this (its takes have held; owner, 18 Oct 2026); travel is first-shot only in every scene anyway. A take whose first shot fails is cancelled and the next seed starts, at about a third of the cost of a full take (owner, 17 Oct 2026). Shots 2 and 3 then carry the scene's surer material; the scene still ramps to its last shot.

**The front scene.** Anything large and fixed in the keyframe gets worked here first. Facing moves front → three-quarter → side and never turns fully away. Its last shot strikes a front double biceps and releases it into a relaxed stance (see *The subscriber close*).

**The back scene.** Its keyframe should already show him turned away: a turn-away from a front-facing keyframe pushed the camera in or swung the set in 5 of 6 takes (12 Oct 2026, Equinox). When the back scene's keyframe shows his back, phase one of its first shot releases that keyframe's hands and feet and he stays turned away. Only when the keyframe shows his front does phase one turn him away: a turn in place to face the wall, the sea or the far end of the set, with no step back: written that way it held 4 of 4 takes with no cut (the camera reframed smoothly behind him, which the owner does not count as a fault), where a step back plus a turn moved the camera in 5 of 6 (12 Oct 2026). A back-facing keyframe is still preferred. Every back-scene shot stays turned away for the whole shot (a turn to camera brings the front view back and wastes the scene), writes the glute contraction in its middle phase, and ends with the head over the shoulder, face clear, eyes in the lens. Framed medium full to full, the whole posterior in frame. The middle-phase glute contraction may be any of the turned-away glute moves in *The pose vocabulary* (the squeeze held at full contraction, the hip flick, the slow grab); a hand on a glute is a grab or a squeeze with the fingers together, never a spread. **Every back scene shows a glimpse, never a full reveal.** H3 is good at glimpses and sneaks of the hole and struggles to draw it completely; the back rounds that went wrong were the ones that tried to show too much (the spread wording in a medium bend drew the wrong anatomy; the medium bend failed the owner's anatomy check in 5 of 6 takes), while the deep wide squat with no spread drew it right and shipped at 5 (waterfall, 16 Oct 2026). So every back scene carries at least one glimpse beat: a slight parting that comes from a stance, a squat or the release of a squeeze, held only for a moment. Glimpse beats that fit: the release of a glute squeeze in a wide stance, so the glutes relax and part slightly · the deep wide squat with no spread, glutes square to the lens, when the floor and the frame allow it (see *Check what the keyframe puts under him*) · a brief upright spread: standing tall with no bend, both palms part the glutes briefly, just for a moment, then let go and move to the hips (untested on H3: judge it on the first round). Never a spread held as a pose, a spread during a bend or a squat, palms pulling the cheeks wide apart, or palms resting on the glutes with the fingers spread: those set up a full reveal that does not come, and the owner docked 3 of 3 takes with that move (17 Oct 2026, night round). Never write a beat whose point is the full reveal. **The glimpse goes in the back scene's first shot, and only there.** Shot N.1 carries the glimpse beat, so a take can be judged after its first ten seconds and cancelled early when the glimpse does not draw, instead of after all thirty (owner, 17 Oct 2026). Shots 2 and 3 of the back scene carry the rest of the glute work (the grab, the hip flick, the squeeze, the poses, the over-the-shoulder look) and nothing whose point is a glimpse or a reveal: no spread, no deep squat, no bend or step that opens the glutes.

**The calisthenics scene.** Standing or squatting moves, nothing on the ground: jumping jacks, shadow boxing, standing calf raises, wall push-ups against a surface the keyframe shows, high knees, a lunge with both feet placed, a squat written as a shape. **Reps at real speed are untested on H3:** moves compress to about two seconds and holds stretch, so a run of reps may not land at speed. Test this first; until it is proven, write a calisthenics shot as two or three reps with the last one held, never a continuous run. Its first shot turns him from its keyframe's facing to the move's angle as its phase-one travel, when they differ. Never the same move twice in the scene.

**The kinds, one per shot:** STANDING POSE (a named physique pose, hands placed) · PROP BRACED (leaning, bracing, pushing against something large and fixed) · HINGED (an arch, a fold, a lunge or a squat driven to full range, written as a shape with the feet placed) · CALISTHENICS (standing reps) · WALK (a reposition that is itself the display: to a place the keyframe already shows, in a scene's first shot only, ending there; toward the lens in any shot when the keyframe has headroom for it; no travel in shots 2 and 3 of a scene). In the front and back scenes no kind in more than two consecutive shots, and at `{{COUNT}}` 6 or more the first six shots carry at least three kinds. A block (one activity in one place) may run two or three shots only if the same activity develops; two different poses are two blocks.

**THE DIRECTOR'S SHOT: the second shot of every scene is yours.** Shots 1 and 3 of a scene follow the structure; shot 2 (1.2, 2.2, 3.2…) is a free slot. The question it answers: **"what would Mizer have this model do with this exact set?"** Inside four limits: the scene's parameters hold for the whole shot (front scene never shows the backside, back scene never turns to the lens, calisthenics scene is standing reps) · it is built on the keyframe's **signature element** (the surf, a rail, a pool edge, a doorframe, a shower), named first, and it is never a kneel, never a stretch, never a move from the vocabulary lists, never the kind of either neighbour; if any keyframe could have produced it, it is not a director's shot · Mizer register with the thirst-trap charge, never a candid chore · the handoff is normal, ending in the scene's facing with the late look. Because it is shot 2, there is no travel: the element must already be where shot 1 ended, so its shape is <he braces on it, leans into it or lets it act on him>, <feet placed, held>; shot 1 reaches the element, which the keyframe already shows, and ends at it. Invent it from this frame; never reuse one from a previous film. A scene too short for a shot 2 has none; when shot 2 is the final shot of the film, *The finish* governs.

**THE SUBSCRIBER CLOSE: a scene's last shot plays to his own phone.** On every scene's last shot that is not the final shot of the film: **the step-in** (front and calisthenics scenes, and only when it fits the frame rule, see *Every beat's end shape must fit inside the keyframe's frame*): in the final phase he steps straight toward the lens until he fills the frame waist-up and sets, never closer; it is a subject move, the camera sentence stays locked, and the next scene's first shot opens at the house shot from its own keyframe. On a tight keyframe there is no step-in: the close happens in place, with the hands, the pose and the look carrying it. The back scene skips the step-in (its full-body framing holds) and plays the close with the hands and the look. **Two hand paths** in sequence, one per phase, the other hand anchored each time: a palm dragged up the abs to the chest, then both hands to the band at the front. **The front scene closes on a FRONT DOUBLE BICEPS, released:** when the step-in fits, the middle phase strikes the front double biceps, fists by the ears, squeezed at the peak, and releases it to the hips (two moves); the final phase is the step-in alone, until he fills the frame waist-up, and the look lands as he sets. On a tight keyframe with no step-in, the middle phase strikes the pose and the final phase releases it into the relaxed stance, hands to the hips or a palm flat on the chest, as the look lands. Either way the end-hold describes the relaxed stance. The pose replaces the middle-phase hand path. **Every scene's last shot and every end-hold land on a relaxed stance, not a flex:** any scene's last shot that uses a named physique pose strikes it in the middle phase and releases it in the final phase. On a tight keyframe the raised fists also need the headroom check: if they would leave the top of the frame, the pose is a front lat spread, fists at the back of the waist, instead. **A longer look:** the look may arrive at the start of the final phase rather than the final second.

**THE FINISH: the final shot of the film is the biggest thing in the film.** Its first two phases are a free approach of any standing kind this keyframe supports, a different kind from the shot before. Its final phase is a travelling move into a **beefcake pinup pose of your own choosing**, built from something in this keyframe, held longer than any hold in the film (it is a relaxed pose already, not a flex), and the film cuts on it. Three rules: never the back double biceps, never the plain arms-at-sides hero stance, and it arrives by travel (a turn or a sideways step counts; a walk toward the lens only when the frame rule allows), never by standing still and striking it. The final shot of the film is exempt from the scene's facing rule and the closing-look rule: he may turn back to the front even when the film ends on the back scene (the turn brings the photo's front view back, which is exactly right here), and his eyes are free, on the lens or off frame. Still no sign-off: no wave, no salute, nothing resolving in open air. With a fixed prop, it is worked into the final position, not released.

**The waistband across the film:** at most one per shot, never in two consecutive shots. **One or two reset gestures across the film, at least one a break: a shake-out of the arms or a roll of the shoulders while resetting the feet; never a laugh, never a breath.** A written "laughing once, short" drew a laugh the owner called weird or off in 3 of 5 takes (17 Oct 2026). Resets go in a release or transition, never in the final shot of the film. **Never repeat a named pose across blocks.**

## The charge: AMG structure, charged delivery

Physics is the hard constraint; the AMG sensibility is how you select among believable continuations. The register is **jock energy**: wide stances, weight thrown onto one leg, hands planted or hooked at the band, an unhurried tempo, a look that arrives late and holds. Mood words buy nothing on H3; the only heat that reaches the render is heat written as a physical action.

**Allowed, and pushed as far as it goes:** the fullest flex, the widest lat spread, the longest line · muscle under real strain at the peak of a pose, struck and squeezed inside one phase · the obliques, lower abs and glutes displayed, which is what the garment exists to leave uncovered · the weight dropped hard onto one leg, a wide stance with the pelvis forward, a step that carries the hips (a hip roll or tilt in place never renders, so never rely on it) · fabric moved by the body (a reach lifts the band, a hinge pulls it taut over the glutes) and by both hands at the front once per shot · a late look into the lens, held to the cut from a relaxed stance · sweat and wet skin when the keyframe already has them.

**Work the garment to the ceiling (see *The job*).** A body you can almost see beats one you can, so the band, the leg opening and the fabric under tension are the levers, driven by the body where possible and by both hands at the front once per shot.

**THE WAISTBAND: the primary heat lever, one per shot, earned.** It is the EXIT from a real gesture, never the entry and never the whole shot. **Never to the side. It always goes down, whatever the garment.** A one-thumb pull "at the hip" drew as a sideways stretch of the band in 2 of 3 takes and slid to the centre front in the third (14 to 15 Oct 2026, Equinox), and the owner does not want sideways pulls at all. A briefs band drawn up taut pulled the briefs up too high in 2 of 5 takes (17 Oct 2026, night round), and the owner wants the band to go down only. Two patterns: facing the camera, any garment, both thumbs hook the band at the front of the hips and draw it DOWN evenly across the hip bones, and hold · **The front of the garment always covers the genitals.** The band may go as low as it can while the fabric still covers them completely, and never far enough to show the genitals or the skin just above them: a white brief drawn down across the hip bones showed a wrong-anatomy line under the band in 7 of 7 takes (owner, 18 Oct 2026, vines round). Write the stop point: "draws it down evenly to just below his hip bones, the front of the briefs still covering him", never an open-ended "down" · turned away, glutes squeezed, thumbs push the band down at the small of the back and hold with the top of the glutes clear. Always both hands at the front, never one thumb at the side, never pulled outward or sideways. No band pull while he sits or is folded at the hips; it renders only standing or turned away. **Write the displacement small and specific** ("draws the band down evenly across his hip bones and holds it there"); a big vague pull smears the garment. The anchor's "the briefs stay on" sentence is what makes this safe.

**Hands: give them a job.** Planted on the hips, braced on a surface, driving a pose, hanging still with the arm straight. One hand path per shot as the exit (two in a scene's last shot and the director's shot, in different phases, never at once): a palm dragged up the abs and planted flat on the chest, a forearm wiped across the brow and dropped to the hip, a palm down the flank. One hand travels while the other stays anchored, and every hand ends ON a surface (a hip, the band, the chest, a wall), never in mid-air. Motivation is the whole difference between body worship and preening: the hand arrives because the body just did something.

**The charge check, every shot.** Each shot has one named peak and enough of these for its position: three for a scene's first shot, three or four for its middle shot, four or more for a scene's last shot and the final shot of the film, from these six. 1 weight dropped hard onto one leg or a wide stance with the pelvis forward · 2 the waistband worked as the exit, both hands at the front, always down, never sideways · 3 a pose struck and squeezed at its peak inside one phase, then released into something new · 4 a late look into the lens, held · 5 light working the body (a keyframe property) · 6 the step-in, when it fits (a scene's last shot only). If a phase could be captioned "here is a correct pose," rewrite it until it could be captioned "look what he can do, and he knows you're watching."

## The pose vocabulary

A named pose renders far more reliably than a vague tightening, but **H3 renders a named pose as its simplest version unless the hands are placed**: a front lat spread drew as hands on hips. So every named pose carries its hand placement. Facing camera: **front double biceps** (fists by the ears) · **front lat spread** (fists at the back of the waist, elbows driven wide) · **hands-on-hips stance, thumbs hooked at the band** · **abdominal-and-thigh pose, one arm overhead, the other hand on the hip**. To the side: **side chest** (near palm on the far pec, hip pushed to camera) · **side triceps** (far hand gripping the near wrist behind the back). Turned away: **back lat spread** (fists at the back of the waist) · **over-the-shoulder back pose** · **rear three-quarter stance, near glute to camera** · **a flick of the hips that makes the glutes jiggle** (untested on H3: write it as a quick hip flick to one side and back, glutes loose, and judge it on the first round) · **a slow sensual grab: one palm closes over the near glute and squeezes it, fingers together, the other hand on the hip** · the standing glute squeeze held at full contraction (under standing work). Never a held spread or a pull that sets up a full reveal; the glimpse comes from a stance, a squat, a released squeeze or a brief upright spread let go at once. Standing work: **overhead reach and full extension** · **a lunge, both feet placed, back heel lifted** · **a standing calf raise held at the top** · **a brace against a column or wall with the weight thrown onto one hip** · **a standing glute squeeze held at full contraction**.

Charged variants, preferred: **front lat spread with the weight dropped onto one leg** · **wide low stance, feet outside the shoulders, pelvis forward, thumbs hooked at the band** · **side chest with the near hip pushed toward the camera** · **overhead reach that lets the shorts ride low** · **brace with the weight thrown hard onto one hip**. One named pose per phase, two per shot at most. **A pose lasts one beat:** it is struck and squeezed at its peak inside one phase, and the next phase releases it into something new. The same pose never spans two phases, and no phase "holds the pose harder", "squeezes harder" or keeps a pose the previous phase struck; a flex held across two phases and into the end-hold read as frozen (a front double biceps held about 7 s to the cut, 14 to 15 Oct 2026). Name the release and where the hands land.

## Phrasing that H3 honours

- **Write the shape, not the verb.** "Hinges forward" drew a 20° lean; a fold written as "<end shape> until <angle or landmark>, <where each hand is placed>, and holds for a count of <n>" drew the fold. Give the end shape, an angle or landmark, the hand placement and a hold.
- **Put a hand in every torso move.** A twist with nothing for the hands vanished 7 of 9; a palm over the pec landed 9 of 9. The right elbow pulls back toward the camera as the torso turns, the left hand planted on the hip.
- **Place the feet.** Name where each foot is before a lunge, a wide stance or a step, or the move collapses into something else.
- **One spinal plane per phase.** Fold OR twist OR arch, never two at once. Unfold first, then turn, in separate phases.
- **Release the keyframe's starting state in phase one of a scene's first shot.** Name what the hands and feet are doing in the first frame and move them: "the man in the dark briefs brings his hands forward from behind his back and plants them on his hips."
- **Every phase ends in a held position**, written ("and holds," "and sets," "and locks it"), and never back in the position the shot started from: returning the hands to where they began reads as giving up the pose. Land somewhere new.
- **Destinations, not paths.** Name the pose and the surface a hand lands on; never narrate the arm's route through the air. Move one arm at a time and say the other stays put, except for symmetrical poses, which are named and not choreographed.
- **Name the eyes in every phase.** Phases one and two go to the work: his own arm, his chest, his hand, the surface he braces on, a spot straight ahead just past the camera when the work has nothing to look at. The target must be in front of his face for the way he is facing. The ground gets one second at most. Never the lens before the final phase, and the lens only late in that phase, after its own action, so the look lands as an event.
- **Never write the face as a state.** No grinning, smirking, brooding, heavy-lidded, and no expression attached to a hold. A break is a physical event that decays (he shakes out his arms).
- **No breathing, in the picture or the sound.** H3 draws the audio with the picture, so a breath anywhere moves his mouth: a written "huffs a breath out" plus breath sounds in the soundscape drew heavy open-mouth breathing, the mouth opening and closing through the take, in 2 of 4 takes (14 to 15 Oct 2026, Equinox), and the owner rated it a fault members notice. No breath, gasp, pant, sigh or exhale in any phase, no breath sound in any soundscape, no wiping sweat from the brow as a mood beat.
- **No invitation to cut.** Never end on a glance toward, a reach for, or a step toward something outside the frame, and never name a nearby object in the closing sentence. The close is a held state inside the frame.
- **Present tense, concrete physical actions only.** Subject's own left and right. No metaphor, no atmosphere, no words that name the medium ("the clip," "the frame," "we see") beyond the two tested phrase and end-hold strings.
- **Persistence.** Anything that should keep happening (surf rolling, a fan turning) is stated as continuous; H3 stops what it is not told to continue.

## The variety review: compare with the last three films

Members watch film after film; a new model doing the same routine reads as the same video. So before the shot list is final, read the beat sheets (`scenes/scene-N/shot-M.txt`, or `shot-list.md`) of the **last three films** (the newest round folders under `test/`, then `test/archive/`) and lay the new film beside them, scene by scene and shot by shot, in a table. Flag anything that repeats: the same named pose, the same hand move (a grab, a palm to the chest), the same order of moves inside a scene, the same closing pose, the same kind of director's shot. Rework every flagged beat into something those films did not do, built from this keyframe. **What the review never changes:** the safeguards that make a take work (the back scene's squat glimpse in its first shot, the camera pull-back on a front-facing back keyframe, the frame rule, the garment rules, the facing rules); keep those on purpose and vary the choreography around them. Include the comparison table in the reply after the shot list, as `<<<FILE variety-review.md>>>` … `<<<END>>>`, so the owner sees what is new before any take runs (owner, 18 Oct 2026, after three back scenes in a row ran turn, squat, brace and grab, hip flick, turn back).

- **STYLE**: one sentence starting `Live-action`: `Live-action physique film, single hard key, locked-off tripod.`
- **CAMERA**: `The camera holds a perfectly static shot throughout the entire ten-second duration: no cut, no dissolve, no transition and no change of framing; the man, the set and the lighting in <Picture 1> stay as they are for the whole video.` A scene's first shot verbatim apart from the subject noun; shots 2 and 3 drop `in <Picture 1>`. Name only what is in frame, never "the props" when there are none. The camera never moves in this skill; the step-in is the subject moving.
- **ANCHOR**: 120 to 200 words describing the keyframe exactly as a STATE, in reading order: the man (build, hair, beard, skin, the garment with colour and cut, anything worn), then the set front to back with colour, material and position, the light (source, direction, quality), the framing (crop, camera height). It ends with the persistence sentence: `The <garment exactly as the photo shows it> stays on and in place for the whole video.` **No pose and no changing state in the anchor**: not where his hands are, not his stance, not an object he will move. Those belong to phase one. For shots 2 and 3 of a scene the anchor states the previous shot's end frame as fact (where he is, facing, framing, wet or dry) and the carry-over opener follows it.
- **CARRY-OVER OPENER** (shots 2 and 3 of a scene): `For the first moment the <man by appearance> holds where he stopped, <the previous shot's exact end pose>, then moves.`
- **PHASE ONE**: `In the first seconds, the <man by appearance> …` Releases the starting state, then the real action underway, arriving at a held position, eyes on the work. Two moves at most.
- **PHASE TWO**: `Through the middle of the clip, the <man by appearance> …` The main gesture driven to full range, the effort in the working muscle, squeezed at the peak and held inside this phase only, eyes still on the work. Two moves at most.
- **PHASE THREE**: `In the final seconds, the <man by appearance> …` An action of its own (the release, the hand path, the waistband, the step-in), then `and in the final second` the head comes square (or over the shoulder in the back scene) and the eyes reach the lens. Two moves at most; a walk-in is the whole phase.
- **END-HOLD**: one sentence, always: `He holds there, <position>, eyes level into the lens, as the clip ends.` The position is a relaxed stance, never a flex (over the shoulder in the back scene; the final shot of the film's eyes free).
- **SOUNDSCAPE**: 1 to 4 sentences, diegetic only, in time order, matching the action: the setting's own tone plus one or two sounds his movements make (water, feet, hands on a surface); never his breathing, laughing, panting or any mouth sound. End with `No voices, no dialogue, no music.`

Example windows, a front scene's last shot: `[0:03-0:07] To show you what he has: fists beside the ears, front double biceps, squeezed at the peak; eyes on his right arm` and `[0:07-0:10] To come down and catch you looking: releases the flex, hands to his hips, stands easy; the look lands in the lens`.
